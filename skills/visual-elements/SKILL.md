# Extract the visual elements

Turn the story into a complete list of things that must be designed. Anything
not on this list will be improvised differently in every shot.

Read `canon.md` first.

## What to list

**Main characters** — build, species, face, age, bearing, how they move.

**Supporting characters** — including crowds. Design a crowd as one group
sheet, not as individuals, so the group stays consistent.

**Costume and accessories** — every garment, its material and colour, plus
anything carried or worn habitually.

**Locations** — interiors and exteriors separately. Note which recur, because
those need to survive re-dressing across seasons or times of day.

**Props** — separate the *hero props* (objects the plot turns on, which appear
in several shots and must be recognisable) from set dressing. Hero props get
their own reference sheet.

**Weather, season and time of day** — as a **progression**, beat by beat. Say
which direction it moves and never let it move back.

**Cultural and architectural detail** — building materials, roof shapes,
ornament, textiles, instruments, food. This is what makes the film feel like
the place rather than anywhere.

**Background elements** — what fills the world: livestock, vehicles, tools,
vegetation.

## Continuity marks — do this deliberately

For each main character, choose **one unmistakable identifying detail** that
appears in every shot — and **make it asymmetric.** A scar over the *right*
eyebrow. A glove on the *left* hand only. A satchel on one shoulder, a patch on
one knee, one rolled sleeve, a notch in one ear.

Asymmetry is the whole point, for two reasons and the second is the real one:

1. It gives the model a hard landmark to preserve, and identity drift is worst
   for characters described only in symmetrical terms.
2. **It converts drift from a judgement into a binary check.** "The face looks
   a bit off" is unfalsifiable and gets waved through. "The scar is on the left
   in shot 7 and the right in shot 4" is objectively wrong, visible in a
   contact sheet, and impossible to argue with.

A mirrored mark is also a *diagnosis*: it means the model flipped the frame,
which means the screen direction went with it.

For each hero prop, choose a **distinctive marking** so the audience recognises
the same object and you can tell when the model has swapped it. Asymmetric
there too — a chip on one rim, a repair on one side.

Also decide and record:

- The **size relationship** between characters, stated plainly ("A is a head
  taller than B, and twice his bulk")
- Each character's **colour temperature** so they stay separable in any light
- A **landmark** that anchors wide shots, and which side of frame it sits on

## Ask about what you do not understand

Where the story uses a word, object or custom you cannot confidently picture,
**ask the user** — do not design a guess. Group the questions and ask once.
Offer your best understanding alongside each question so the user can correct
rather than explain from scratch.

## Output

Append the full inventory to `canon.md` under a Continuity section, and seed
`assets.md` with every item that will need a reference image, in the order they
should be generated:

1. Style keyframe
2. Scale sheet (all leads together, with articulated hands)
3. Individual character sheets
4. Environment plates
5. Hero props

Present the list and confirm before generation begins.
